# HOW MUCH TRAINING VOLUME DO YOU ACTUALLY NEED?

**Tier:** Free · Public Resource
**Category:** Muscle (Hypertrophy) Education
**Read time:** ~5 minutes

---

"Train each muscle once a week" used to be the default bodybuilding wisdom. Then the pendulum swung hard toward "train everything constantly." The honest answer sits in the middle, and it depends far more on total weekly volume than on how many times you touch a muscle group in a given week.

## What "Volume" Actually Means

Training volume is the total amount of hard work you do for a muscle group, usually counted as **sets per muscle group per week**. A set of 10 reps on chest press and a set of 12 reps on cable fly both count as one set toward your weekly chest volume, even though they're different exercises, what matters for growth is the accumulated, challenging sets across the week, not any single workout in isolation.

## The General Range That Matters

Research and real-world coaching experience converge on a useful, general range for most lifters:

- **Roughly 10-20 hard sets per muscle group per week** is the zone where most people see solid, sustainable growth.
- **Below about 10 sets/week**, a muscle group is typically under-stimulated for someone actively trying to build it, enough to maintain what you have, but a slow path to new growth.
- **Above about 20-25 sets/week**, more volume tends to produce diminishing returns, and for most people it starts costing more in recovery than it adds in stimulus, this is a ceiling to approach carefully, not a target to chase by default.

Where you personally land in that range depends on training experience (newer lifters generally need less volume to grow and can be overwhelmed by more), how well you recover, and how many muscle groups you're training in the same week. This is a general-population starting range, not a fixed prescription, an individualized plan adjusts it to you.

## Frequency: Once a Week Isn't Enough for Most People

Frequency is how often you train a given muscle group across the week. The old "one brutal session per muscle per week" approach has fallen out of favor for good reason: research on muscle protein synthesis (the process your body uses to actually rebuild and grow tissue) suggests it stays elevated for roughly 24-48 hours after a hard session, then tapers. Training a muscle group only once a week means long stretches where that growth signal has already faded before the next session hits it again.

**A more effective general approach: hit each muscle group at least twice per week.** This doesn't mean two identical brutal sessions, it more often looks like splitting your weekly volume for a muscle group across two (or more) sessions, which also means each individual session can be a bit less exhausting while the weekly total stays the same or even increases.

## Why More Frequent, Smaller Sessions Often Beat Fewer, Bigger Ones

Spreading the same weekly volume across more sessions tends to produce better quality per set, you're less fatigued walking into each session, so your form, your mind-muscle connection, and the intensity you can safely apply all improve. A chest that gets 18 sets crammed into one exhausting Monday session is working with worse conditions, set-to-set, than a chest that gets those same 18 sets spread across two well-recovered sessions in the week.

## What This Means for You

- Total weekly sets per muscle group matters more than which single workout they happen in.
- A general, useful starting range for most people is roughly 10-20 hard sets per muscle group per week.
- Training each muscle group at least twice a week generally beats once a week, because it keeps the growth signal from muscle protein synthesis more consistently elevated.
- More volume isn't automatically better past a certain point, recovery capacity, not ambition, is the real ceiling.

*This article is educational and general in nature, not individualized medical or fitness advice. Volume and frequency needs vary by individual, a coach can help you find the right starting point for your experience level and recovery capacity. Talk to your doctor before significantly increasing training volume if you have an underlying medical condition or are returning from injury.*
