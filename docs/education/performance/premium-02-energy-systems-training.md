# ENERGY SYSTEMS TRAINING: MATCHING CONDITIONING TO ACTUAL DEMANDS

**Tier:** Premium · Member Vault
**Category:** Performance Education
**Read time:** ~7 minutes

---

"Conditioning" gets treated as one generic thing — get on the bike, do some intervals, get tired. Real performance conditioning is more specific than that: different sports and goals draw on different energy systems in different proportions, and conditioning work that doesn't match those actual demands can leave an athlete gassed in exactly the moment that matters, despite being "in shape" by every gym metric. This is where the base model's Day 2 and Day 5 conditioning blocks get personalized to what a client actually needs.

## The Three Energy Systems, in Practical Terms

Your body has three systems for producing the energy (ATP) that fuels movement, and they hand off to each other based on how hard and how long an effort lasts:

- **Phosphagen system (ATP-PC):** powers maximal efforts lasting roughly 0-10 seconds — a single sprint, a max jump, one all-out play. Recovers relatively quickly (60-90% restored in about 2-3 minutes) but only fuels very short bursts.
- **Glycolytic system (anaerobic):** takes over for efforts lasting roughly 10 seconds to 2 minutes — repeated sprints, a hard interval, a wrestling match period. This is the system responsible for that burning, "can't sustain this" sensation, and it needs longer recovery (2-5+ minutes) to substantially clear before it can repeat at full output.
- **Oxidative system (aerobic):** powers everything beyond about 2 minutes, and also underlies how quickly you recover *between* short, hard efforts in a stop-and-start sport. A better aerobic base means faster recovery between sprints, not just better distance-running capacity.

Almost no real sport or goal draws on just one of these — the question is the *proportion*, and that proportion is what should drive the conditioning work in the program.

## Matching Conditioning to the Actual Demand

This is the single biggest lever for personalizing the Performance base model's conditioning blocks, because "athletic conditioning" looks very different depending on what it's actually for:

| Athlete/Goal profile | Dominant system(s) | Practical conditioning protocol |
|---|---|---|
| Sprinter, jumper, powerlifter-adjacent | Phosphagen | Very short, maximal efforts (5-10s) with full recovery (2-3+ min) — quality over density |
| Field/court sport (soccer, basketball, football) | All three, repeated-sprint pattern | Repeated short sprints (10-20s) with incomplete recovery (30-60s), building the ability to repeat hard efforts without full recovery between them |
| Combat sport, wrestling | Glycolytic-dominant | Interval work matched to round length (e.g., 2-3 min hard, 1 min rest), plus repeated shorter bursts within that window |
| Distance-based sport or general work capacity goal | Oxidative, with anaerobic layered in | Longer steady-state or tempo work as the base, with intervals layered in as the specific demand requires |
| General athleticism, no specific sport | Balanced across all three | Rotate emphasis across the week — one day short/maximal, one day repeated-effort, one day base aerobic work |

## Real Work:Rest Protocols

Translating the table above into actual session structure:

- **Phosphagen-dominant work:** 5-10 second maximal efforts (sprints, sled pushes, jump variations), rest 2-3+ minutes between reps. Total volume stays low — 6-8 reps — because the whole point is every rep at true maximal output. This is the protocol used in the base model's Day 2 acceleration sprints.
- **Repeated-sprint / mixed work:** 10-20 second hard efforts, 30-60 second rest, repeated for 6-10 rounds. This trains the specific "sprint, recover just enough, sprint again" demand of most field and court sports — closer to game-realistic recovery windows than full recovery would be.
- **Glycolytic-dominant intervals:** 20-90 second hard efforts at a sustainable-but-hard pace, rest roughly equal to or slightly longer than the work interval (a 1:1 to 1:1.5 work:rest ratio), for 6-10 rounds. This is the base model's Day 5 battle ropes/assault bike interval pattern.
- **Aerobic base work:** longer continuous efforts (20-40+ minutes) at a conversational-to-moderate pace, or longer tempo intervals (3-5 minutes work, 1-2 minutes rest). Builds the aerobic engine that speeds recovery between the harder, shorter efforts above — genuinely useful even for athletes whose sport is dominated by short bursts.

## Why This Matters More Than "Just Get Tired"

Two athletes can both finish a conditioning session exhausted and have trained completely different things. A field-sport athlete doing long steady-state cardio is building an aerobic base that has some value, but it's not training the repeated-sprint capacity their sport actually demands — a session of true repeated-sprint intervals will transfer far better, even though it might "feel" less grueling by the clock. Matching the protocol to the actual demand is what makes conditioning time-efficient instead of just tiring.

## Building This Into the Base Model

The Performance base model's Day 2 (Speed/Agility + Conditioning) and Day 5 (Conditioning + Core/Stability) blocks are written with a general anaerobic-capacity finisher as the default — the biggest single personalization lever is swapping that default protocol for the work:rest structure that actually matches the client's sport or goal, using the table above as the starting reference point.

## What This Means for You

- Conditioning isn't one generic thing — different sports and goals draw on different energy systems in different proportions, and the training should match.
- Short, maximal efforts with full recovery build a different quality than repeated sprints with incomplete recovery, which build a different quality than sustained aerobic work — know which one your goal actually needs.
- Field and court sports usually need repeated-sprint-style conditioning (short efforts, incomplete recovery) more than they need long steady-state cardio.
- A good aerobic base still matters even for explosive, short-burst sports — it's what speeds recovery between the hard efforts that actually decide the outcome.

*This article is educational and general in nature, not individualized coaching advice. High-intensity interval and sprint-based conditioning is demanding on the cardiovascular system and joints — if you have a heart condition, are new to this intensity of training, or have any relevant health concerns, talk to your doctor before starting, and stop and seek medical attention for any chest pain, dizziness, or unusual shortness of breath during training.*
