# HOW TO ACTUALLY STRUCTURE A POWER/SPEED TRAINING BLOCK

**Tier:** Premium · Member Vault
**Category:** Performance Education
**Read time:** ~7 minutes

---

You already know from the free-tier content that strength feeds power, power feeds speed, and quality beats volume on every explosive rep. This is where that turns into an actual block structure — the phases, the real numbers, and the progression logic a coach uses to build a power/speed block that gets someone measurably faster and more explosive over 8-12 weeks, not just tired.

## The Three-Phase Structure

A well-built power/speed block moves through three phases, each with a distinct job. Skipping a phase — especially jumping straight to peak-intensity work — is the single most common way this kind of training produces injuries instead of results.

**Phase 1 — Movement Prep and Base (roughly weeks 1-3)**
The job here is technique, not output. Landing mechanics, sprint start position, deceleration control, and general strength are built and grooved at submaximal intensity. Volume is moderate, intensity is controlled (RPE 6-7), and the athlete is coached on absorbing force correctly — a quiet landing, knees tracking over toes, full-foot contact — before jump height or sprint speed become the focus at all. Athletes who skip this phase are the ones who get hurt in Phase 2 and 3, because the block starts asking for more output than their mechanics can safely absorb.

**Phase 2 — Power Build (roughly weeks 4-7)**
Now the intent shifts to genuinely explosive output. Strength work moves to lower rep ranges (4-6) with real intent to move the bar fast, plyometric volume increases, and power exercises (box jumps, trap-bar jumps, med-ball throws) are trained at or near maximal effort on every rep. Rest between power sets extends to 90-150 seconds — full recovery, not a conditioning pace. This is where the actual power adaptation (rate of force development) gets built.

**Phase 3 — Peak Power (roughly weeks 8-10)**
Volume comes down, intensity and specificity go up. Strength work tightens to 3-5 rep ranges at higher loads, power work is trained at true maximal intent with the longest rest periods in the block (150+ seconds), and — where relevant — conditioning work shifts to mirror the actual energy-system demands of the athlete's sport or event (see the energy-systems article for the specifics). This is the phase where competition- or event-specific qualities peak.

**Taper (roughly week 11) and Retest (week 12)**
Volume drops sharply while intensity/intent stays high — the goal is arriving at the retest fresh, not fatigued. Retest the same benchmarks measured at the start of the block (vertical jump, sprint time over a fixed distance, a relevant strength number) to give both the athlete and the coach real data on what the block produced.

## Real Intensity Progression, Numbers You Can Use

For the strength work underneath the power block, a practical progression across the three phases:

| Phase | Rep range | Load (%1RM, approx.) | Rest | Bar-speed intent |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — Base | 8-10 | 65-75% | 90s | Controlled, technique-focused |
| 2 — Build | 4-6 | 75-85% | 120s | Fast concentric on every rep |
| 3 — Peak | 3-5 | 85-90%+ | 150s | Maximal intent, full recovery |

For the power/plyometric work itself, progression isn't primarily about load — it's about **volume of quality reps** and **complexity**:

- **Phase 1:** lower box heights, sub-maximal jumps, simple patterns (double-leg jump, basic med-ball chest pass). 3-4 sets of 3-5 reps.
- **Phase 2:** full-height box jumps, added external load on some variations (weighted jump squats at light load), more complex patterns (single-leg elements, rotational throws). 4 sets of 3-5 reps.
- **Phase 3:** highest-intent variations, sport-specific patterns where applicable, lowest volume of the block (fatigue management matters most here). 3-4 sets of 3-5 reps at absolute maximal intent.

## The Non-Negotiable Progression Rule

Across every phase, power work is progressed by **quality, not fatigue**: every rep is performed at or near maximal effort with full recovery between reps, and a set — or the whole exercise for that session — is cut the moment jump height, throw distance, or bar speed noticeably drops. This is the opposite of how accessory strength work gets progressed (where working to a controlled level of fatigue is often the point). Confusing the two — grinding out power reps for volume the way you'd grind out a strength set — is the fastest way to turn a power block into a technique-degradation block.

## Building the Block Around the Individual

The three-phase structure is the skeleton; a few levers adjust it for the person in front of you:

- **Training age:** a newer athlete may need 4-5 weeks in Phase 1 instead of 3 — technique competency gates the move to Phase 2, not the calendar.
- **Sport/event demands:** the specific movements and energy-system emphasis in Phase 3 should mirror what the athlete actually needs (a sprinter's Phase 3 looks different from a field-sport athlete's).
- **Injury history:** a prior hamstring, knee, or ankle issue shifts more time and attention into Phase 1's movement-prep work before any true maximal-intent output is introduced.

## What This Means for You

- A power/speed block has three real phases — base/movement prep, power build, peak power — and skipping the base phase is where most avoidable injuries come from.
- Strength work underneath the block follows a fairly standard load progression; power work is progressed by output quality, not load or fatigue.
- Full recovery between power reps (90-150+ seconds depending on phase) is a feature, not wasted time — it's what keeps every rep at true maximal intent.
- Retesting the same benchmarks you measured at the start of the block is what turns "I think I got faster" into an actual, trackable result.

*This article is educational and general in nature, not individualized coaching advice. Power, plyometric, and sprint training carry real injury risk, especially without an adequate strength base or sound landing/sprint mechanics — this kind of programming should be built and supervised by a qualified coach, and any joint pain or sharp pain during this work should be evaluated by a clinician before continuing.*
