# MONITORING FATIGUE AND AVOIDING OVERTRAINING: THE REAL MARKERS

**Tier:** Premium · Member Vault
**Category:** Performance Education
**Read time:** ~7 minutes

---

The free-tier content covered why recovery is the limiting factor in performance training. This is where that becomes an actual monitoring system — the concrete markers worth tracking, the thresholds that mean "back off," and the specific difference between normal hard-training fatigue and the early stages of overtraining, so you catch it as a dip instead of a setback.

## Normal Fatigue vs. Overtraining: The Real Distinction

Every good training block produces fatigue — that's not a warning sign, it's the stimulus working. The distinction that matters:

- **Functional overreaching:** planned, short-term accumulation of fatigue (a hard week or two) followed by a lighter week, after which performance rebounds *higher* than before. This is intentional and productive — it's how the Power Build phase of a block is supposed to feel by its later weeks.
- **Non-functional overreaching / overtraining:** fatigue that accumulates without adequate recovery to absorb it, where performance doesn't rebound — it keeps declining, sometimes for weeks, even with reduced training. This is the outcome the monitoring system below exists to catch early, while it's still a quick fix instead of a multi-week setback.

The marker that separates the two isn't how hard a week felt — it's whether performance and how you feel bounce back after a planned lighter period. If it does, that was productive overreaching. If it doesn't, something needs to change.

## Concrete Markers Worth Tracking

**Performance markers (the most objective signal you have):**
- **Jump height or a simple power benchmark** (e.g., a standing broad jump), checked periodically, not every session. A sustained drop of roughly 5-10%+ from your recent average, across more than one session, is a real signal.
- **Sprint time over a fixed, familiar distance** — same logic. Sessions naturally vary a little; a sustained downward trend doesn't.
- **Bar speed / rep quality on power lifts** — even without exact bar-speed measurement, a coach or the athlete can usually feel when reps that used to move fast start moving noticeably slower at the same load.

**Physiological markers:**
- **Resting heart rate**, checked first thing in the morning before getting up. A rise of roughly 5-10+ beats per minute above your personal baseline, sustained across several mornings, is a meaningful signal — more useful as a trend than any single day.
- **Heart rate variability (HRV)**, if you have access to a device that tracks it — a sustained downward trend is one of the better early-warning signals available, though day-to-day noise means it's read as a trend, not a single reading.
- **Sleep quality and duration** — both how much and how restorative it feels. Fatigue that isn't recovering well often shows up here first, before it shows up in performance numbers.

**Subjective markers (don't underrate these — they're often the earliest signal):**
- **Session RPE creeping up** for the same prescribed load or output — the workout on paper hasn't changed, but it feels harder than it did two weeks ago.
- **Motivation and mood** — dreading training you'd normally want to do, or irritability that doesn't have an obvious outside cause.
- **Persistent, low-grade soreness or heaviness** in the legs that doesn't clear with a normal day of rest.

## What to Actually Do, in Order

1. **Confirm it's a pattern, not a day.** One rough session or one bad night of sleep isn't a signal — 3+ days or 2+ sessions showing the same trend across multiple markers is.
2. **Check the obvious inputs first.** Has sleep genuinely dropped? Has life stress spiked? Is nutrition (especially total calories and protein) actually supporting the training load? Most early fatigue red flags trace back to one of these three before they trace back to "too much training."
3. **Take a planned lighter day or two** — reduce volume and intensity, keep movement light, prioritize sleep. This alone resolves the majority of early fatigue flags.
4. **If markers don't rebound within a few days of reduced load,** pull back further — a full deload day or, in a more persistent case, moving the block's scheduled deload week earlier than planned.
5. **Persistent decline despite reduced training load** is worth a conversation with a doctor, particularly if it's paired with symptoms beyond training performance (illness frequency, mood, sleep disruption) — this moves outside what training adjustments alone can fix.

## Building Monitoring Into the Program, Not Bolting It On

The most sustainable version of this isn't a complicated daily questionnaire — it's a short, consistent habit: a quick morning resting heart rate check, a simple 1-10 "how ready do I feel" rating before training, and a coach or the athlete themselves periodically retesting the same jump/sprint benchmark. Combined with just paying attention to sleep and mood, this catches the large majority of overtraining risk early, without turning training into a data-entry job.

## What This Means for You

- Fatigue itself isn't the enemy — planned, temporary overreaching followed by recovery is how performance actually improves. The problem is fatigue that doesn't rebound.
- Track a small set of markers consistently (a performance benchmark, resting heart rate, sleep, and a simple readiness rating) rather than trying to monitor everything.
- A sustained trend across multiple markers is a real signal; a single rough day is not.
- When the signals say back off, backing off is what protects the block's progress — pushing through is what turns a two-day dip into a multi-week setback.

*This article is educational and general in nature, not individualized medical advice. Persistent performance decline, elevated resting heart rate, disrupted sleep, or other symptoms that don't resolve with reduced training load and adequate rest should be evaluated by a doctor — this content is not a substitute for that evaluation.*
