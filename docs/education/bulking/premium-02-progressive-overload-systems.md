# PROGRESSIVE OVERLOAD SYSTEMS: HOW TO ACTUALLY DRIVE GROWTH WEEK TO WEEK

**Tier:** Premium · Member Vault
**Category:** Bulking Education
**Read time:** ~7 minutes

---

You know from the free-tier content that progressive overload — gradually increasing the demand on a muscle over time — is the engine behind long-term growth. "Add weight when you can" is the idea in one sentence, but it's not a system, and without a system it's easy to stall, add weight too aggressively and wreck your form, or plateau without realizing why. Here are the actual methods coaches use, with real numbers.

## Method 1: Linear Load Progression

This is the method the Bulking base program uses by default, and it's the right starting point for most lifters in a surplus: **add load every 1-2 weeks on the primary compound of each day, as long as every rep is completed with good form.**

**In practice:**
- Upper body compounds (bench press, overhead press, rows): add 2.5-5 lb when the prescribed reps are hit cleanly across all sets.
- Lower body compounds (squat, deadlift, hip thrust): add 5-10 lb, since lower-body muscles generally tolerate bigger jumps.
- If a rep target isn't hit cleanly two sessions in a row at the same weight, hold there another week rather than pushing forward on schedule.

**Why it works well for bulking specifically:** you're in a surplus, which means more recovery resources are available for adaptation than during a deficit — a straightforward, aggressive-when-possible load progression is appropriate because recovery isn't being split between building and cutting.

**Where it breaks down:** linear progression naturally slows as weights get heavier and closer to your real strength ceiling — a beginner can often add weight almost every session; an intermediate lifter several months into consistent training usually can't, which is when it's time to shift to one of the methods below.

## Method 2: Double Progression

Instead of adding load every week regardless of reps, double progression uses a rep *range* and only adds weight once you've hit the top of that range for every set.

**Example:** a set prescribed at 8-12 reps.
1. Start at a weight you can hit for 8 reps across all working sets.
2. Keep that weight and add reps week to week until you hit 12 reps on every set.
3. Once you hit 12 reps on every set, add weight (typically 5-10%) and drop back down to around 8 reps at the new weight.
4. Repeat.

**Why it's useful:** it removes the guesswork of "should I add weight today" — the rep count tells you. It's also more forgiving on days when energy or sleep isn't ideal, since you can still make progress (via reps) without needing to hit a new load number that particular session.

**Best fit:** accessory and isolation work (lateral raises, curls, leg extensions, most 10-15 rep accessory movements in the base program) where chasing pure load progression on every single exercise isn't realistic or necessary — this is the method to layer onto the base program's accessory movements even while running linear progression on the main compounds.

## Method 3: Volume Progression (Adding Sets)

When load progression stalls but you're still recovering well and gaining, adding a set to a movement is a legitimate way to keep increasing total training stimulus without needing a heavier weight that day.

**Example:** if Barbell Bench Press has been programmed at 4 sets for several weeks and load progression has slowed, adding a 5th set for a block (then bringing it back down around a deload) increases total weekly volume — one of the core drivers of growth — without changing the load progression scheme itself.

**Caution:** this is the easiest method to overdo. Adding sets increases the total recovery cost of a session, and the base program's higher-volume-than-cutting design already asks a lot of recovery capacity. Add volume in small increments (one set to one or two key exercises at a time) and watch joint feel and session-to-session recovery closely.

## Method 4: RPE-Based Progression

Rather than a fixed rep or load target, RPE (Rate of Perceived Exertion, roughly "how many more reps could you have done") lets effort guide the session. The base program's periodization table already uses RPE targets by phase (RPE 6-7 in Phase 1, climbing to 8-9 in the Peak phase) — this method means adjusting the day's actual weight up or down slightly to land in that RPE window, rather than rigidly hitting a pre-written number regardless of how the session feels.

**Best fit:** more experienced lifters who've built an accurate sense of their own effort, and any lifter during weeks where sleep, stress, or general recovery is clearly off — RPE-based adjustment lets you keep training hard without forcing a number your body isn't ready for that specific day.

## Putting It Together Across a 12-Week Block

A practical way to combine these across the base program's periodization:
- **Phase 1 (Base Volume):** linear progression on compounds, double progression on accessories — building working weights and volume tolerance.
- **Phase 2 (Load):** linear progression continues on compounds where it's still working; shift to RPE-based micro-adjustments where linear jumps start failing two sessions running.
- **Phase 3 (Peak):** RPE-anchored loading on compounds (targeting the prescribed 8-9 RPE), volume held steady rather than added — this is a loading phase, not a volume-accumulation phase.
- **Deload:** no progression — the point of this week is recovery and reassessing strength numbers for the next block.

## What This Means for You

- Linear load progression is the right default for most of the base program's main compounds, especially earlier in a training career.
- Double progression is the better fit for accessory and isolation work, and for anyone whose linear progress has started to stall.
- Adding sets (volume progression) is a real lever, but the easiest one to overdo — add it carefully and watch recovery.
- RPE-based adjustment becomes more valuable as training experience grows and is essential for adjusting a hard day without abandoning the plan.

*This article is educational and general in nature, not individualized medical or coaching advice. Stop and consult a clinician for any joint pain, sharp pain, or pain that doesn't resolve between sessions — never push load progression through reported pain.*
