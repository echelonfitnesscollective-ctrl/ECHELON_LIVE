# SIZING YOUR SURPLUS FOR LEAN GAINS: THE ACTUAL NUMBERS

**Tier:** Premium · Member Vault
**Category:** Bulking Education
**Read time:** ~7 minutes

---

You know from the free-tier content that a moderate surplus beats a massive one. This is where that turns into an actual, personalized number, how to find your real maintenance calories, how to size the surplus precisely, and how to tell early whether it's dialed in or already running too hot.

## Step 1: Find Your Real Maintenance Calories

The same principle that applies to fat loss applies here, just in reverse: a formula gives you a starting estimate, but tracking gives you the truth.

1. Track your food intake honestly for 1-2 weeks without deliberately changing anything.
2. Track your weight daily during that window and use the trend (average), not single days.
3. If your weight stayed roughly flat, your average intake during that window is close to your real maintenance level.

Formulas like Mifflin-St Jeor are a reasonable starting estimate if you need a number on day one, but your own tracked data, from your actual body, at your actual activity level, will always be more accurate than a population average.

## Step 2: Size the Surplus: Moderate, Not Maximal

Once you have a real maintenance number, build the surplus from there:

- **Conservative surplus:** 10% above maintenance. Best for someone who's already fairly lean and wants to prioritize keeping the gain as clean as possible, or someone who has struggled with fat gain on previous bulks.
- **Standard surplus:** 15% above maintenance. The sustainable default for most intermediate lifters, meaningful fuel for growth without an excessive rate of fat gain.
- **Higher surplus:** 20% above maintenance. Reserved for newer lifters (who can build muscle faster and put a larger surplus to good use) or naturally hard gainers who've shown they don't gain fat quickly even at a bigger surplus.
- **Avoid surpluses beyond roughly 20-25% above maintenance** for extended periods, past that point you're not meaningfully accelerating muscle gain, you're just accelerating fat gain, for the same reasons covered in the free-tier "dirty bulk" article.

**In real numbers:** a 180 lb intermediate lifter with an estimated maintenance of 2,600 calories would land on a standard surplus of roughly 2,900-2,990 calories/day (15%), not 3,600.

## Step 3: Set a Target Rate of Gain, and Use It as Your Real Gauge

Percentages and calorie counts are useful for planning, but the number that actually tells you whether the surplus is working is your rate of weight gain:

**Target: roughly 0.25-0.5% of body weight gained per week.**

For a 180 lb lifter, that's about 0.45-0.9 lb per week, slow enough that the gain stays predominantly muscle, fast enough to see real progress over a 12-week block. This matches the weekly check-in target used in the Bulking base program itself.

- **Newer lifters** can often run at the higher end of that range (or even a bit above it in the first several weeks) since their muscle-building rate is naturally faster.
- **More experienced lifters** should lean toward the lower end, the more trained you are, the slower your realistic ceiling for new muscle gain, and any excess above that ceiling is fat.

## Step 4: Know When the Surplus Is Running Too Hot

Watch for these signals, roughly in order of how early they show up:

1. **Rate of gain is meaningfully above 0.5%/week for 2+ consecutive weeks**, especially after the first couple of weeks (which often include some water weight and glycogen loading that isn't a true signal yet).
2. **Waist circumference or visible midsection fat is climbing noticeably faster than strength and muscle are visibly improving.** Some fat gain during a bulk is normal and expected, a waist that's growing out of proportion to the muscle being built is the specific red flag.
3. **Energy and digestion feel consistently heavy or sluggish**, which is more common with an oversized surplus built on low-quality, low-satiety foods than with a well-structured moderate one.

**What to actually do, in order:**
1. **Recheck adherence first.** Confirm the tracked intake is still accurate, surpluses drift upward easily as people get more comfortable and portion sizes creep.
2. **Trim the surplus by roughly 5-10%** if the rate of gain is genuinely running hot and adherence checks out.
3. **Reassess maintenance** every 6-8 weeks or after a meaningful amount of weight gained, a heavier body generally has a higher maintenance level, so the same absolute calorie number represents a smaller surplus than it did at the start.

## Step 5: This Only Works With Training and Protein in Place

Sizing the surplus correctly assumes the other two pillars are already covered: progressive overload driving the training stimulus (covered in the next article in this series) and sufficient protein intake protecting the ratio of muscle to fat gained. A well-sized surplus without enough training stimulus still mostly builds fat, the surplus creates the *opportunity* for growth, training and protein are what actually direct it toward muscle.

## What This Means for You

- Find your real maintenance number through tracking, not just a formula, before sizing a surplus off of it.
- Start with a standard 15% surplus for most intermediate lifters; adjust up or down based on training age and how your body actually responds.
- Target roughly 0.25-0.5% body weight gained per week as your real-world gauge, not the calorie number on paper.
- When the rate of gain runs hot, check adherence first, then trim the surplus modestly, don't overcorrect.

*This article is educational and general in nature, not individualized medical or nutrition advice. If you have a medical condition, a history of disordered eating, or are taking medication, talk to your doctor or a registered dietitian before making significant changes to your calorie intake.*
